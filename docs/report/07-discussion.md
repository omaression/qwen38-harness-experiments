# VI. Discussion and Threats to Validity

## A. What the decisions mean

The controlled arms show that the gate does what its rule says: it promotes a candidate that is not worse, rejects one that is, and names the gates responsible (H1), and CI reproduces every decision exactly (H2). Experiment A shows what the rule means on a real model at small suite sizes. A margin of 0 rejected an adapter that changes nothing, on the evidence of one reply per split, and the significance guard correctly did not fire. Conversely, a real dev-split gain of four items was promoted but could not be confirmed, and the held-out split could not have confirmed any gain because the base model was at ceiling. Both outcomes are visible in the reports themselves (McNemar's p, $\mathrm{MDE}_{80}$ and the warning on the number of losses needed for significance), which is the purpose of reporting them with every decision.

Experiment C repeats the lesson on a suite three to four times larger and with a no-op arm recorded on purpose. The rerun of the bf16 arm disagreed with the first recording on one to three items per split and was rejected on both dev splits. The ternary model's bug-fix discordance against bf16 was of the same size, three items over both splits, all of them losses. The gate at margin 0 therefore rejected the ternary model and the no-op on evidence of the same size: one or two items per split, none significant. What separates them is the direction of the discordant items, not their number, and three items in one direction cannot reach significance.

**Noise floor and effect size.** The size of an effect has to be compared with the measured noise floor, not with zero. On the 74-item held-out bug fix the no-op's interval is [-0.041, +0.041] and the minimum detectable effect is 0.053. The ternary model's loss is -0.027 against bf16 and -0.041 against the engine control, both inside the no-op's interval. The suite can neither confirm nor exclude a true loss of the observed size, 3 to 4 points. A margin near 0.05 is therefore the defensible setting for this suite and stack. A smaller margin rejects changes that the suite cannot tell from a rerun, and every Experiment C comparison would pass `primary` at 0.05. The margin is a product decision about how much loss is acceptable, but it should not be set below what the suite and the serving stack can resolve.

Two practical rules follow. First, before a real comparison, a no-op arm should be recorded against the baseline to measure the serving noise floor, and the margin should be set from it, or promotion should additionally require that a loss be significant. Experiment A put that floor at one discordant item in 13 to 21 on the demo suite, and Experiment C at one to three in 41 to 148 on HumanEvalFix. Second, a held-out split on which the baseline scores at or near 1.000 cannot rank candidates, and a gain observed elsewhere should not be reported as confirmed until a harder or larger held-out suite is available.

**The ternary model against its publisher's figure.** The ternary model's held-out bug-fix rate is 0.97 of the bf16 rate (71 against 73 of 74), close to the 98.2% of the full-precision benchmark average that PrismML reports for it [31]. The two numbers are not like for like. The publisher's figure averages 14 benchmarks in thinking mode at its recommended sampling settings, with its own serving setup. The figure here is one 74-item suite at temperature 0 and medium reasoning effort, with the published Q2_0 file on stock llama.cpp. The interval of the difference, [-0.069, +0.000], is consistent with both no loss and a loss of several points. The result is therefore consistent with the reported retention. It does not confirm it.

## B. Internal validity

**Serving nondeterminism.** The identity adapter is exactly equal to the base model in exact arithmetic, yet it changed replies. The serving stack in Experiment A applied the adapter through LoRA kernels, batched requests and used speculative decoding (`experiments/exp-a-qwen38/README.md`); each can change floating-point reduction order, and greedy decoding amplifies a single changed token into a different reply [14], [15]. Experiment C measured the same effect without any adapter: two recordings of one bf16 model on one engine, at temperature 0 and seed 0, differ on 8 of 345 items. Neither experiment can attribute the change to a mechanism, because each recorded one sample per item and varied nothing else. Every difference between arms in this report therefore includes a nondeterministic component, which C0 measures for Experiment C's bf16 stack only. The llama.cpp arms had no rerun, so their own noise floor is not measured.

**One sample per item.** Each arm holds one reply per item at temperature 0. A second recording of the same arm could differ on some items, as C0 shows, and the paired analysis treats the recorded reply as the arm's fixed answer. The bootstrap interval reflects sampling of tasks, not sampling of replies.

**Engine confounds (Experiment C).** vLLM and llama.cpp differ in kernels, batching, sampling implementation, chat-template rendering and the parsing of reasoning output. A comparison of a ternary model under llama.cpp with a bf16 model under vLLM confounds the weights with the engine, and the Q8_0 arm under llama.cpp is meant to separate the two. C1 lost no bug-fix item, so there is no sign that the engine caused C3's bug-fix losses, and C2 shows the same losses. The control cannot exclude an engine effect of that size, though: at 115 bug-fix items an engine effect of 2 to 3 items, the size of C0's 3 discordant bug-fix items, could not be detected, and the llama.cpp noise floor was not measured. The separation is partial. C1 also includes 8-bit rounding. C2 compares two different weight sets, because Ternary Bonsai 2 is published as its own model built from Qwen3.8-27B [31] rather than as a rounding of the same weights, so C2 measures the published ternary model and not ternary rounding as such. Q8_0 against bf16 under the same engine was not recorded, so the 8-bit rounding and the engine change within C1 are not separated either.

**Recording conditions.** Both arms of Experiment A were recorded on the same server with identical settings, and all arms of Experiment C with identical request settings, so differences in settings do not enter the comparisons. The manifests do not record the hardware, the load or the order of requests during each recording. The median latencies of the llama.cpp arms (20 to 55 s, against 4 to 6 s for vLLM) are therefore not evidence about either engine. Greedy decoding, chosen so that every arm decodes the same way, is not the ternary model's recommended setting. Its publisher recommends sampling at temperature 1.0 in thinking mode [31], so the ternary arm may be measured below its intended operating point.

## C. External validity

The demo suite consists of 34 small, synthetic, single-function Python tasks. Experiment A evaluates one model family, one base checkpoint (a community fine-tune rather than the stock release) and two adapters. Experiment C evaluates the stock checkpoint, one ternary model, one 8-bit conversion and two engines. Real bug fixing spans files and repositories with their own environments [8]; the harness's structure (paired items, execution labels, the gate and the ledger) carries over, but its sandbox does not, and per-task containers would be needed. HumanEvalFix, the suite of Experiment C, is also single-function Python. Its 115 kept tasks are not a random sample of its 164: tasks whose bug shows only beyond the docstring's examples, or whose tests are floating-point or truthiness asserts, are left out (`tasks/humanevalfix/README.md`, section 4). The findings about the gate's behaviour on small suites are expected to generalise; the absolute scores are not.

## D. Construct validity

**Format and capability are confounded in the primary metric.** Six of the seven discordant items in Experiment A are format or truncation failures (Section V-D), and no bug-fix reply that was valid failed a test. In Experiment C, two of the ternary model's three bug-fix losses against bf16 are a truncated reply and a reply that is not valid JSON, and five of the no-op's eight discordant items are format failures. The resolved rate and the judge's balanced accuracy, as defined, measure whether the feature produces a usable answer, which is what a product receives, but a reader interpreting them as reasoning ability would be misled. A per-item classification of failures, as in Sections V-D and V-F, should accompany any interpretation.

**The thinking window.** The 1 to 1,200 word window and the 16,384-token limit turn long reasoning into failures. They encode a product decision about latency and cost, not a property of correctness; another product could set them differently and obtain different verdicts. The ternary model's only truncations in Experiment C, on HumanEvalFix one bug-fix and one judge reply, and one more on the demo judge, are losses that this limit creates.

**Token cost.** The gate does not price tokens, and a candidate that is not worse can still cost more. On the held-out bug fix the ternary model used 1.60 times the completion tokens of bf16, and 1.18 to 1.35 times on the judge, while on HumanEvalFix the 8-bit engine control used about the same tokens as bf16 (0.94 to 1.00 times). A deployment that moves to the ternary model to save memory therefore pays part of the saving back in generated tokens per request. The reports carry the cost as a warning, and the product has to decide whether it is acceptable.

**Test adequacy.** "Resolved" means that the shown and hidden tests passed. Hidden tests reduce, but do not eliminate, the acceptance of wrong patches [18]. HumanEvalFix's converted hidden tests are fewer than the original asserts, so a patch that passes them may still fail a dropped assert.

**The judge's task.** The judge is evaluated on exactly two patches per task, the reference and one wrong fix that passes the shown tests. This design isolates whether the judge reads the patch, but it does not cover other kinds of wrong patch, and its labels are only as good as the tests. On HumanEvalFix the construction is also optimistic. The wrong patch is the buggy code behind a visible guard that special-cases the shown inputs, so a judge can reject it by spotting the guard without understanding the bug (`tasks/humanevalfix/README.md`, section 8). In Experiment C no arm accepted a wrong patch: the true negative rate is 1.000 except for one invalid reply. Every wrong verdict was a correct reference patch judged incorrect. The judge comparisons of Experiment C therefore measure false rejections and format failures, and they say little about whether a judge detects subtle bugs.

**Static analysis.** The scan resolves common aliasing but cannot see a name constructed at run time, so the absence of a block-class finding is evidence, not proof, of the absence of the construct.

## E. Conclusion validity

**Small suites and low power.** With 13 to 148 items per comparison, the reported $\mathrm{MDE}_{80}$ lies between 0.018 and 0.210, and McNemar's test needs at least six losses with no gains to call a regression significant [11]. A promotion is therefore evidence of non-inferiority at the stated margin, not of equality, and a rejection by one to three items is not evidence of a regression. No comparison in either experiment reached significance; the smallest McNemar p in Experiment C is 0.25.

**Interval and test can disagree.** On the judge dev split of Experiment A the bootstrap interval of $\Delta$, [+0.038, +0.269], excludes 0 while McNemar's exact p is 0.125. With four discordant items, all in one direction, the exact test cannot reach significance, whereas a percentile bootstrap over only 13 clusters, whose resamples contain no loss at all, can understate the uncertainty at such sizes. In Experiment C the no-op's held-out judge interval, [+0.000, +0.047], rests on three gains and no loss in the same way. The gate decides on neither alone; the report gives both, and neither should be cited without the other.

**Ceiling effects.** When the baseline scores 1.000, $\Delta \le 0$ is certain and any change can only tie or lose. Two of the four comparisons in Experiment A have a baseline at ceiling. On the demo suite the bf16 arm of Experiment C scores 1.000 on every split. All three arms resolve every demo bug-fix item, and the only three rejections there come from single format failures on the judge. The demo suite cannot rank these arms; only the larger HumanEvalFix suite shows the ternary model's bug-fix losses. On HumanEvalFix itself, four of the eight bf16 and Q8_0 arm-splits are at 1.000 (bf16 on the dev bug fix; Q8_0 on both bug-fix splits and the dev judge).

**Multiple comparisons.** Experiment A comprises four decisions and Experiment C 28, 16 on HumanEvalFix and 12 on the demo suite, without correction. Each decision is reported as a separate gate verdict and not as a family-wide claim. With so many decisions, a few rejections of comparisons that change nothing are expected, as C0 shows.

**Post hoc hypotheses and reuse of the held-out split.** H3 and H4 were written in their present form after Experiment A's replies existed. They are the predictions implied by the arms' construction (an exact identity and a trained adapter), but they were not registered in advance. H5 and H6 were stated in the plan before Experiment C was recorded, for the bug fix; the plan is not among the repository's files and fixed no margin, so H6 is tested at margin 0. The rerun arm, the judge feature, the demo replication, the retention ratio and the reading at margin 0.05 have no prior hypothesis and are exploratory. The demo suite's held-out split has been used for the synthetic arms and for Experiments A and C, and HumanEvalFix's held-out split for the four arms of Experiment C. The ledger counts held-out evaluations and warns after ten distinct candidates, a heuristic budget rather than a guarantee of the kind studied by Dwork et al. [21].

**Contamination.** The demo suite is public in the repository, so a model trained on the repository could have seen its held-out split; its held-out status protects against prompt tuning, not against training on the internet. HumanEvalFix derives from HumanEval, published in 2021 and widely copied, and may be in the pretraining data of the models compared in Experiment C [19], [20]. Held out here means held out from prompt tuning, not unseen by the model. Contamination raises absolute scores, and the near-ceiling bf16 bug-fix rates (0.986 and 1.000) are consistent with it, although they do not demonstrate it. It could also interact with quantization if memorised solutions survive weight reduction differently from reasoned ones; a paired comparison removes neither effect. A ternary model that keeps memorised HumanEval solutions would look better on this suite than on unseen bugs.
